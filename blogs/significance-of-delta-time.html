The Significance of Delta Time

August 2026•10 min read•Game Design
Back to all posts

There's a variable that shows up in almost every game loop ever written, usually named something boring like dt or deltaTime, and it rarely gets a second thought once it's wired in. But delta time is one of those quiet, foundational ideas that separates a game that behaves consistently from one that turns into a slideshow-powered physics glitch the moment someone's frame rate drops. I've been meaning to write this one down for a while, mostly because I keep re-learning the same lessons about it every time I touch a new project.

What delta time actually is

At its simplest, delta time is the amount of real-world time that passed between the current frame and the previous one — usually a fraction of a second, like 0.016 for a smooth 60fps frame or 0.033 if you dropped to 30fps. Every game loop is, underneath everything else, just a loop that keeps asking "how long did that last frame take?" and using the answer to scale whatever happens next.

The reason this matters so much comes down to a simple fact: your computer does not update the game at a fixed, guaranteed rate. Frame rate fluctuates constantly — a busy shader, a garbage collection pause, another app stealing CPU time, a weaker device altogether. If your game logic assumes every frame takes exactly the same amount of time, you've quietly tied your entire simulation to whatever hardware happens to be running it.

The world before delta time (and why it broke)

It's worth remembering why this became standard practice in the first place. Early games often moved things by a fixed amount per frame — move the player 5 pixels every update, full stop. That works fine as long as the frame rate never changes. The moment it does, though, everything built on that assumption falls apart: a game tuned on a fast machine turns into slow motion on a slower one, and a game tuned on a slow machine turns into a blur on a fast one. Famously, this is part of why some older PC ports of console games ran hilariously fast on modern hardware — the simulation speed was hard-wired to an assumed frame rate that no longer matched reality.

Delta time is the thing that lets a game say "move 5 units per second" instead of "move 5 units per frame" — and only one of those phrases means the same thing on every machine.

The basic fix: multiply, don't assume

Once you're scaling movement, rotation, timers, and animation by delta time, a game becomes frame-rate independent — the player moves at the same real-world speed whether the game is rendering at 30fps or 240fps, because each frame's movement is scaled down or up to compensate. Instead of writing:

  • position += speed (frame-dependent — speed varies with frame rate)
  • position += speed * deltaTime (frame-independent — speed is measured per second, and every frame contributes its fair share)

It looks like a trivial change, and mechanically it is. But the implications reach into almost every moving part of a game: physics integration, camera easing, particle lifetimes, cooldown timers, AI decision intervals, even UI animations. Anything that changes over time should be asking delta time how much time has actually passed, rather than assuming.

Where it gets trickier than "just multiply it"

This is the part that took me longer to actually internalize, because the naive version of delta time scaling works right up until it doesn't.

1. Physics really wants a fixed timestep

Physics simulations are sensitive to the size of the timestep in ways simple movement isn't. Feed a physics integrator a wildly varying delta time — say, a huge spike after a frame hitch — and you can get tunneling (an object moving fast enough that it skips clean through a wall in a single step), unstable collision resolution, or springs and constraints that blow up into jittery nonsense. This is why most engines run physics on a separate, fixed timestep (commonly something like 1/60th of a second, stepped as many times as needed to catch up) while rendering and input stay on the variable, real delta time. You decouple "how often does the world get simulated" from "how often does the screen get repainted."

2. Clamping your delta time

If the game window loses focus, a breakpoint hits during debugging, or a big asset streams in and hitches the main thread, delta time for the next frame can spike to something enormous — a whole second, or more. Multiply everything by that unclamped value and a player can teleport through a level, a physics object can fly off into the void, or a timer can silently expire instantly. The common fix is to clamp delta time to a sane maximum (say, no more than 0.1 or 0.25 seconds) so a hitch causes visible slowdown rather than a catastrophic jump.

3. Floating point error accumulates

Delta time is usually a small floating-point number, and repeatedly adding small floating-point numbers over a long play session can drift. It's rarely dramatic, but in long-running simulations — an idle game left open for hours, or a timer that's supposed to track wall-clock time precisely — that drift can matter. Using double precision for accumulated time, or periodically resyncing against a real clock, is a common mitigation.

4. Not everything should scale with delta time

It's tempting to multiply every single value in the game by delta time out of habit, but some things genuinely shouldn't. A one-shot event triggered by an input press shouldn't be scaled by frame duration. Certain UI or menu logic is often deliberately pinned to a fixed update rate for consistency across devices, independent of the game's render framerate. Knowing when not to apply delta time is as much a part of using it well as applying it everywhere else.

Delta time and game feel

There's also a less technical, more "feel" side to this. Once movement, animation blending, and camera follow logic are all properly scaled by delta time, a game tends to feel smoother across a wider range of hardware — which matters enormously for anything shipping on PC, where frame rates can vary by an order of magnitude between machines. A dash that covers a fixed distance in a fixed amount of real time reads as consistent and fair regardless of whether the player is running the game at 45fps or 165fps. Get this wrong, and even subtle inconsistencies — a jump that's slightly floatier at high frame rates, an attack that's slightly faster at low ones — can make a game feel unfair or broken without the player necessarily being able to say why.

What I've taken from all this

The pattern I keep coming back to, across pretty much every system I build, is to treat delta time less like a multiplier you sprinkle on at the end and more like a fundamental unit the whole simulation is expressed in from the start. Movement, timers, animation, AI think-intervals — I try to write all of it in terms of "per second" quantities from the outset, rather than retrofitting delta time scaling in after the fact once something starts behaving strangely on a different machine. It's a small discipline, but it saves a specific, annoying category of bug: the one that only shows up on someone else's hardware, at a frame rate you never personally tested at.

Takeaway: delta time is what lets a game describe motion in real seconds instead of arbitrary frames. Get the basic scaling right and your game behaves consistently across hardware. Get the edge cases right — clamping, fixed-step physics, knowing what shouldn't scale — and it stays consistent even when a frame hitches, a device struggles, or a session runs for hours.

It's a small variable carrying a disproportionate amount of responsibility. Most players will never know it exists, which, honestly, is the whole point — if delta time is doing its job, the game just feels right, on whatever machine happens to be running it.
